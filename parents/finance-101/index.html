Personal Finance Basics

A plain-language tour of money, for grown-ups

1. How money flows

Gross pay is what you earn before anything comes out. Take-home (net) pay is what lands in the bank after deductions: federal and state income tax withholding, FICA (Social Security 6.2% and Medicare 1.45%), and anything you elect, like health insurance premiums or 401(k) contributions.

Budget from take-home pay, not gross pay. Your pay stub shows every line; reading one closely once a year is a great habit.

The simple order of operations most guides agree on: cover essentials, build a starter emergency fund, grab any employer 401(k) match, pay off high-interest debt, finish the emergency fund, then invest more for the long term.

2. Budgeting

Zero-based budget

Every dollar of take-home pay gets a job: bills, food, savings, debt, fun. Income minus planned spending equals zero. It does not mean spending everything; savings is a job too.

50/30/20 rule

A quick starting point: about 50% of take-home pay to needs (housing, utilities, groceries, insurance, minimum payments), 30% to wants, 20% to savings and extra debt payoff. Adjust it to real life; it is a guide, not a law.

Budgeting with biweekly pay

Biweekly means 26 paychecks a year, so most months get two checks and two months get three. A reliable method:

  • Build the monthly budget on two regular checks only.
  • Assign each bill to the paycheck that comes before its due date (a simple "paycheck calendar").
  • Treat the two "third paycheck" months as bonus months: send that check to savings, debt or a planned big expense.

Variable income (commission, bonuses)

  • Base the budget on the lowest realistic amount, such as your lowest commission check from the past year, or your base pay alone.
  • When a bigger check arrives, send the extra by a set rule, for example: top up a "smoothing" buffer first, then split between savings and debt.
  • Commission checks are often withheld at a flat supplemental rate, so the tax taken out may not match what you actually owe. Check at tax time.

Reimbursements (like mileage)

A mileage reimbursement pays you back for using your own car, so treat it as car money, not extra income: fuel, maintenance, tires and future replacement. Parking it in a separate "car" savings account keeps it honest. Under an accountable plan these reimbursements are generally not taxable wages.

3. Emergency fund

An emergency fund is cash for surprises: a car repair, a job gap, a medical bill. A common target is 3 to 6 months of essential expenses, more if your income varies (commission) or one income supports the family. A starter goal of $1,000 or one month of expenses is a good first milestone.

Keep it in a high-yield savings account at an FDIC-insured bank (or NCUA-insured credit union), insured up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. It should be boring and easy to reach, not invested in stocks.

4. Debt and credit

APR (annual percentage rate) is the yearly cost of borrowing. A credit card at 24% APR charges roughly 2% a month on the balance you carry. Paying the full statement balance each month means you pay no interest on purchases.

Avalanche vs snowball

Pay minimums on everything, then put all extra money on one debt:

  • Avalanche: highest APR first. Saves the most interest.
  • Snowball: smallest balance first. Quick wins that keep you motivated.

Either works if you stick with it; the comparer below shows the trade-off.

Credit score basics

FICO scores range 300 to 850 and weigh: payment history (about 35%), amounts owed / utilization (30%), length of history (15%), new credit (10%) and credit mix (10%). Pay on time every time, keep card balances low (many aim under 30% of limits, lower is better), and avoid opening lots of new accounts at once. You can get free credit reports from all three bureaus at AnnualCreditReport.com.

5. Saving and investing

Compound interest

Growth earns growth. Money growing 7% a year roughly doubles in about 10 years (the "Rule of 72": 72 / 7 ≈ 10). Starting early matters more than starting big.

Index funds

An index fund owns a whole market slice (like the S&P 500 or the total US market) instead of picking stocks. Low fees and broad diversification make them a common core holding. Stocks can drop sharply in any given year, so money needed within a few years usually belongs in savings, not stocks.

401(k) and the match

If your employer matches, contribute at least enough to get the full match; it is an instant return on your money. Check your plan's vesting schedule.

Roth vs traditional

  • Traditional (401(k) or IRA): tax break now, pay tax when you withdraw in retirement.
  • Roth: pay tax now, qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free.
  • Rough rule: Roth is attractive if you expect a higher tax rate later; traditional if you expect a lower one. Many people use both. Roth IRAs have income limits.

6. Insurance basics

Insurance protects against losses you could not afford on your own. Key words: premium (what you pay), deductible (what you pay before coverage starts), out-of-pocket maximum (your yearly cap on covered health costs).

  • Health: compare total yearly cost (premiums plus likely out-of-pocket), not just the premium.
  • Auto: state minimum liability limits are often low; many advisors suggest higher liability limits to protect your savings.
  • Home or renters: covers your dwelling and belongings plus liability.
  • Life: if others depend on your income, term life is the simple, affordable choice for most families.
  • Disability: replaces part of your pay if you cannot work; check what your employer provides.
  • A higher deductible lowers your premium, but only makes sense if your emergency fund can cover it.

7. Taxes basics

Federal brackets are marginal: each rate only applies to the dollars inside that bracket. Moving into a higher bracket never lowers your take-home pay; only the extra dollars are taxed at the higher rate.

Taxable income = income minus adjustments (like traditional 401(k) contributions) minus the standard deduction (or itemized deductions). 2026 standard deduction: $16,100 single, $32,200 married filing jointly, $24,150 head of household.
